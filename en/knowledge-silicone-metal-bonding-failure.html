1. Core Bonding Mechanisms: Chemical Adhesion vs. Mechanical Interlocking

Silicone rubber is built on a high-energy siloxane (Si-O-Si) backbone with very low surface tension and high chemical inertness. It has virtually no natural affinity for stainless steel, aluminum, carbon steel, or brass. Molding liquid silicone rubber (LSR) or high-consistency rubber (HCR) directly onto bare, smooth metal yields only weak van der Waals contact that easily peels under load or thermal cycling.

Reliable industrial silicone-to-metal overmolding relies on combining two mechanisms:

  • Chemical Bonding: A specialized silane-coupling primer is applied to the metal substrate. During vulcanization under heat and pressure, one end bonds with hydroxyl groups (-OH) on the metal surface while the other crosslinks with the silicone polymer network.
  • Mechanical Interlocking: Controlled micro-roughness (sandblasting) combined with macro geometry (through-holes, undercuts, knurling) allows molten silicone to flow and lock physically around the insert, resisting shear and axial pull-out forces.

2. Five Root Causes of Silicone-to-Metal Delamination

  1. Metal Insert Surface Contamination: Residual CNC cutting fluids, anti-rust oils, fingerprints, or mold-release transfer completely block primer wetting and prevent covalent bonding.
  2. Improper Primer Selection, Coating, or Baking: Platinum-cured LSR/HCR and peroxide-cured HCR require matched primer chemistries. Over-diluted primer, excessive film thickness, or insufficient solvent flash-off causes outgassing voids at the interface during molding.
  3. Substrate-Specific Passivation & Poisoning: Carbon steel rusts rapidly after blasting if priming is delayed; aluminum requires anodizing or conversion treatment to stabilize its dense oxide layer; stainless steel needs uniform surface anchoring; copper and brass alloys can poison platinum catalysts unless isolated with a barrier primer.
  4. Thermal Expansion (CTE) Mismatch Stress: Silicone exhibits a linear CTE around 200–300 × 10⁻⁶ /K compared with ~23 × 10⁻⁶ /K for aluminum and ~11 × 10⁻⁶ /K for steel. Cooling from 150°C–175°C curing temperatures generates high shear stress at sharp metal corners or abrupt wall-thickness transitions.
  5. Mold Insert Shifting & Air Entrapment: Loose locating pins let high-pressure rubber push the insert off-center, starving one side of material, while poor venting leaves micro-voids that initiate peel cracks.

3. Four Mold & Structural Design Countermeasures

Design Measure Engineering Guideline Anti-Delamination Effect
1. Through-Hole Interlocking Add through-holes, slots, or annular grooves (recommended hole dia. ≥ 1.5 mm) Silicone bridges both sides like a molded rivet, resisting axial pull-out.
2. Edge Radii Replace sharp 90° insert edges with R0.3–R0.8 mm chamfers/radii Reduces cooling shear concentration and edge tear initiation.
3. Precision Shut-Off Design 0.02–0.05 mm sealing interference at metal-to-mold shut-off zones Prevents flash onto non-bonded areas and maintains cavity packing pressure.
4. Insert Preheating Preheat heavy metal inserts to 80°C–120°C prior to loading Prevents heat-sink quenching at the bond line so curing completes evenly.

4. Bond Strength Verification & Failure Mode Analysis

  • Axial Pull-Out & 90°/180° Peel Tests (ASTM D429): Measures peak detachment load (N/kgf) or peel strength per unit width (N/mm).
  • Cohesive vs. Adhesive Failure: The desired result is Cohesive Failure (the silicone body tears while a rubber film remains bonded to the metal). Clean separation exposing bare metal (Adhesive Failure) indicates surface prep or primer process defects.
  • Thermal Shock Cycling: Evaluating bond integrity after -40°C to +150°C cycling for automotive, UAV, and industrial environments.

5. FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions)

Q1: What are the most common causes of silicone-to-metal delamination?

The primary causes are metal insert surface contamination (machining oil, oxide layers, mold release residue), improper primer selection, uneven coating or incomplete solvent baking, and lack of mechanical interlocking features.

Q2: How do aluminum, stainless steel, carbon steel, and copper differ in silicone overmolding?

Carbon steel oxidizes rapidly after sandblasting and must be primed promptly; aluminum forms a dense native oxide layer that often requires anodizing or conversion coating; stainless steel needs controlled surface roughening; copper alloys require specialized barrier primers to prevent platinum cure inhibition.

Q3: How is silicone-to-metal bond strength verified?

Bond strength is evaluated via axial pull-out testing, 90-degree or 180-degree peel testing (such as ASTM D429), and thermal shock cycling (-40°C to +150°C). The target failure mode is Cohesive Failure inside the silicone body rather than interfacial separation.
